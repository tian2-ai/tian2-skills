# Scientific Merit — sub-criteria, section map, and report-level differentiators

The Research Report is the primary evidence for the **Scientific Merit** criterion (1 of 4 in
`rubric.json`; each scored /5 in 0.5 increments, equally weighted, total /20). Because the composite
is a SUM, a weak Merit score caps the whole application. You are scored **relative to your category
pool** — category means differ (Chemistry/Physics/Biochem grade harder; Behavioral/Social softer),
and the Z-score normalization corrects for that. So a strong report in a soft-graded field still
needs genuine rigor to stand out on Z.

## The three Scientific Merit sub-criteria (from rubric.json)

1. **Scientific Validity** — sound scientific principles; the work is significant; it advances the
   field. Reviewers ask: is the question real and grounded in the literature? Is the significance
   stated and defensible? Does this push past what was already known?
2. **Scientific Setup** — clear hypothesis / goals; sound experimental or analytical design;
   appropriate controls; adequate sample size. Reviewers ask: can this design actually answer the
   question? Are controls present and named? Is n enough to support the claim?
3. **Scientific Analysis** — appropriate analysis methods; identified sources of error; valid
   conclusions that follow from the data; acknowledged limitations; sensible future directions.
   Reviewers ask: is the analysis right for the data? Are error sources named? Do conclusions
   match the evidence without overclaiming? Are limitations honestly stated?

## Scoring anchors (apply to each sub-criterion)

- **5.0** — exceptional / groundbreaking
- **4.0-4.5** — strong
- **3.0-3.5** — adequate
- **2.0-2.5** — limited
- **1.0-1.5** — weak

Typical composite across the whole application is 9-12; the On-The-Table internal docket (~19% of
entries) sits near a raw average of ~16.4+ and a Z-score around +0.74 at the cut. The point for the
report: a "groundbreaking" Merit score is rare and earned by the differentiators below, not by
length or polish.

## Standard paper structure -> which sub-criterion each section feeds

| Section | Feeds | What reviewers look for |
|---------|-------|--------------------------|
| **Title page** | (excluded from page count) | Clear, specific title. |
| **Abstract** | (excluded from page count) | Accurate, self-contained. Wording help -> `sts-essay-coach`. |
| **Introduction / rationale & significance** | Validity | Question situated in the literature; the gap named; significance stated, not assumed. |
| **Methods (with controls)** | Setup | Design that can answer the question; controls present and named; sample size justified; reproducible. |
| **Results** | Setup + Analysis | Data shown with spread/uncertainty (error bars, CIs), not just means; every figure/table cited. |
| **Analysis (error sources & limitations)** | Analysis | Appropriate methods; error sources identified; a REAL limitations section (2-4 specific items). Depth -> `sts-data-analysis-tutor`. |
| **Conclusions** | Validity + Analysis | Conclusions follow from the data; no overclaiming ("is consistent with" not "proves"). |
| **Future work** | Analysis | Concrete next directions that follow from the limitations. |
| **Bibliography** | (excluded from page count) | The one place links are allowed; cite properly -> `sts-rules-wizard` for style. |

## Report-level differentiators (what moves 16.5 -> 18+)

These are the report-level expressions of the winner differentiators. Coach the student toward them
where their project genuinely allows; never manufacture them.

1. **Cross-validate the key claim with a second independent method.** The single strongest rigor
   signal. If the central result is shown two ways (e.g. a simulation AND an analytic bound, or two
   independent datasets), Validity and Analysis both rise.
2. **Contribute something NEW** — a method, a term, a generalization, or a regime not previously
   tested. Reproducing a known result with an existing tool is not a contribution. Push the student
   to name what is new.
3. **A rigorous NULL result is a winning result** when framed as **shrinking the search space** ("we
   show effect X does not occur under conditions Y, narrowing where it could"). Reframe nulls as
   findings; never bury or apologize for them.
4. **Quantify significance and repeat the number.** A concrete, repeated figure ("76% less power",
   "100 -> 10 keV", "3.2x faster") reads as merit far more than adjectives. Make the headline number
   appear in abstract, results, and conclusion.
5. **An external stamp helps** — publication, first-authorship, top fair, or olympiad — but it lives
   in Task 6, not the report body. Mention only if the student raises it.
6. **Bound the mentor's role and own the question.** The report should reflect the student's own
   work; per-phase attribution lives in Task 4 (`sts-contribution-coach`).

## Merit red flags (cost points — all observed in real winners' contrast cases)

- **"Limitations: None / N/A"** — the single biggest avoidable red flag. A report with no real
  limitations section signals the student cannot critique their own work. ALWAYS require 2-4 specific
  limitations. Treat its absence as a must-fix, not a suggestion.
- **Confirmatory result dressed as discovery** — reproducing something known, presented as novel.
- **Thin data behind a big claim** — small n / single trial / no replication vs sweeping language.
- **Overclaiming impact** ("revolutionize", "proves", "first ever") before the results justify it.
- **Uncited figures** (especially the student's own) — a Merit problem AND a disqualification risk
  (see `report-format-rules.md`).
- **Analysis that doesn't match the data** — wrong test, no error sources, conclusions beyond the
  evidence. Hand the depth to `sts-data-analysis-tutor`.

## Honest framing on scoring

Tell the student the truth: scoring carries large category/evaluator variance (rank-by-Z and
rank-by-raw correlate only r~0.18), and about 19% of entries reach the internal docket. A clean,
rigorous, honestly-limited report with one genuine NEW contribution and one cross-validated headline
number is the realistic target — not a guaranteed-winning paper. Integrity screens (plagiarism,
AI-content, COI) can park even a high-scoring report.
