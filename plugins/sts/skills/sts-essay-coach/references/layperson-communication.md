# Layperson communication — the hook, accuracy-under-simplification, quantify-and-repeat

Reference for `sts-essay-coach`, focused on Essay 1 (the 200-word Layperson's Summary) but the
craft applies to the impact and potential essays too. Grounded in the verified OTT winner analysis
(`STS_Top400_Winning_Criteria.md`): an accessible layperson hook is part of the FLOOR every winner
cleared, and "quantify significance and repeat the number" is one of the six differentiators.

## Why the layperson summary matters disproportionately

- It is the first thing many evaluators read in plain language, and it sets the frame for the
  Overall Scientific Potential read (communication is explicitly part of that criterion).
- It is the most copy-able piece of the application: a quotable line propagates into how the work
  gets described downstream. Winners write a line a journalist could lift verbatim.
- It is also the easiest essay to write generically — which makes it the highest-leverage place
  to demand a real hook and concrete numbers.

## The HOOK — a line a journalist could quote

The opening must give a non-specialist an immediate, vivid handle on the idea. NOT "My project
investigates the effects of X on Y." Instead, an image or contrast that lands in one read.

Verified winner-grade hooks (use as the bar, not as templates to copy):
- "Goldilocks zone" — borrowed a familiar phrase to frame a habitability/optimum idea instantly.
- "If you throw a ball at a wall it bounces back — in the quantum world it doesn't." — a concrete
  everyday picture that sets up the counterintuitive phenomenon being studied (quantum tunneling).

What makes these work:
- **Concrete, sensory, everyday** anchor (a ball, a wall; a fairy tale) before any jargon.
- **One idea**, not three. The hook carries a single surprising claim.
- **Quotable** — it could be the pull-quote in a news write-up without edits.
- **Honest** — the simplification points at the real phenomenon, not a flashier fake one.

How to elicit a hook from the student (do not invent it):
- "What's the one surprising thing a stranger should know about your result?"
- "What everyday object or experience is the closest analogy — and where does the analogy break?"
- "If a reporter wrote one sentence about your work, what would you want it to say?"
Then help shape the student's own answer into a tight opening line. The hook must be true to
their actual finding.

## Accuracy under simplification — the non-negotiable constraint

The summary must be accessible AND scientifically accurate. The failure mode is an analogy that
makes the work clearer but smuggles in a claim that is false. Check every simplification:
- Does the analogy imply a mechanism the research did not show?
- Does "cures / proves / solves" overstate what a single study established?
- Would a specialist in the field wince at the simplified claim? If yes, fix the wording, not the
  science.
A good test: the lay sentence and the technical sentence should be the SAME claim at two
resolutions — never two different claims.

## The four required beats (all must appear in ~200 words)

1. **Background** — the question, in one plain sentence. Why it was open.
2. **Procedures** — what the student actually did, in lay terms. No method-name soup ("I used
   DFT and Monte-Carlo and …"); say what those did in plain words.
3. **Conclusions** — what was found. State the headline result with its number.
4. **Relevance** — why someone outside the field should care. Connects to a real-world or
   field-level stake without overclaiming.

A workable shape: hook (1 sentence) → background (1–2) → procedures (2–3) → conclusion w/ number
(1–2) → relevance (1–2). That fits inside 200 words.

## Quantify significance — and repeat the SAME number

One of the six winner differentiators: quantify the significance and reuse the figure.
- Pick the single most defensible headline number ("76% less power", "100 keV → 10 keV", "3x
  faster", "a 40% reduction").
- Use the SAME number, same units, in the paper, the layperson summary, and the impact essay.
  Three different phrasings of one result reads sloppy and invites doubt; one consistent figure
  reads rigorous.
- If the student does not yet have a clean number, that is a finding to surface — push them to
  compute/state it (with the data-analysis sibling) rather than inventing a figure here.

## Reusing the craft in the other essays

- **Impact (Essay 2):** the same quantified figure anchors the benefit; then stage WHO does WHAT
  next instead of promising a sweeping outcome. "Revolutionize / transform / cure" before results
  is a red flag — replace with a concrete, bounded next step.
- **Potential (Essay 3):** the "hook" becomes a single vivid scene that shows how the student
  thinks; quantify where natural, but the currency here is a concrete moment, not a statistic.
- **Common App (Essay 4):** no jargon to translate, but the same demand for concreteness applies —
  a distinctive, sensory, could-only-be-you detail is the hook.

## Generic / AI-voice red flags to catch in any essay

Push back on these and ask for the specific replacement (evaluators run AI-content detection; the
Ethics Statement certifies the essays were not constructed with AI tools):
- "Since I was young, I have been passionate about science." (generic opener)
- "This research has the potential to revolutionize / transform the field." (overclaim)
- Long strings of method names with no plain-language gloss.
- Sentences that could belong to any applicant's essay — no name, no place, no specific moment.
- A sudden register/voice shift between paragraphs (an AI-assembly tell).
The fix is always the same: replace the generic sentence with the concrete, personal, verifiable
detail the student supplied during intake — in the student's words.
