# Cold email and the ask — outreach playbook (the student writes and sends)

This file gives the student templates and a checklist for reaching a PI or an RRI (Regulated
Research Institution) placement. The skill helps STRUCTURE and CRITIQUE the student's own
draft. It does NOT auto-send, and it does NOT ghost-write the email — the student writes,
personalizes, signs, and sends from their own account. Cold emails are application-adjacent
material; keep them honest.

## Why cold-emailing works for STS

A cold email is not a long shot reserved for the connected. It is a normal, effective access
route, and it has produced STS winners directly:

- One winner cold-emailed a PI at Duke and built the project from that relationship.
- Another emailed a PI at UW–Madison.

The lesson is not "send more emails." It is "make a sharper ask." A specific, narrow,
researchable ask from a student who has clearly read the PI's recent work converts far better
than volume. Most cold emails to busy PIs go unanswered — that is normal and not a verdict on
the student.

## What a strong ask looks like

The ask is the whole email. Get it right and the rest is logistics.

STRONG (specific, small, shows the student already did work):
- "I read your 2024 paper on X. I'm trying to test whether the same effect holds under
  condition Y, which your discussion noted you hadn't examined. Could I have 20 minutes to
  ask whether my approach is sound before I commit a summer to it?"
- "I've built a small pipeline that reproduces your Figure 3 on public data. I'd value 15
  minutes of your feedback on whether my extension to dataset Z is worth pursuing."

WEAK (vague, large, makes the PI do the thinking):
- "Will you be my mentor?" (too big for a first email)
- "I'm very interested in your field. Any advice?" (no ask the PI can act on)
- "I'll work full-time on whatever you need." (offers labor, not a question)

The strong ask does three things: (1) names a SPECIFIC piece of the PI's recent work,
(2) shows the student has already started thinking/working, and (3) requests something small
and bounded (a short conversation), not a commitment.

## Email template (the student fills every bracket honestly)

```
Subject: HS student question about [specific topic] — 20 minutes?

Dear Professor [Last name],

I'm [full name], a senior at [school] in [city]. I read your [year] paper on
[specific paper / finding] and was struck by [the one specific thing — a result, a method,
an open question they named].

I'm working on [the student's specific question in one sentence]. So far I have
[one concrete thing already done — a pilot analysis, a reproduced figure, a dataset
assembled]. I'm trying to decide whether [the specific next step], and I'd value your view
on whether the approach is sound.

Would you be willing to spare 20 minutes by video call in the next few weeks? I understand
you're very busy, and no reply is a fine reply.

Thank you for your time,
[Full name]
[School] · [grade] · [your school email address]
```

## Outreach checklist

Before sending, the student confirms:

- [ ] The email address came from a PUBLIC source (the PI's lab/department page). Never use a
      paid email-finder or scrape a non-public source.
- [ ] The subject line is specific and honest — never "URGENT", never clickbait.
- [ ] The first sentence identifies the student (name, school, grade).
- [ ] Exactly ONE specific reason for emailing THIS PI, citing one of their works.
- [ ] One concrete thing the student has already done is named (proof of initiative — this is
      Initiative under the Student Contribution rubric).
- [ ] The ask is small and bounded (a short call), not "be my mentor."
- [ ] The PI's time is acknowledged; "no reply is a fine reply" included.
- [ ] Sent from the student's SCHOOL email (looks more legitimate than a personal Gmail).
- [ ] No attachments on the first email (offer to send if the PI is interested).
- [ ] One PI per email — never CC multiple PIs.

## Follow-up etiquette

- Wait at least two weeks before a single, short follow-up. One follow-up maximum, then stop.
- If a PI declines or says they don't take HS students, respect it and move on.
- If a PI has a public statement that they don't take HS mentees, don't email about mentorship.

## Programs as access (RSI and similar)

Structured summer research programs place students into real labs and are a legitimate access
route alongside cold-emailing:

- **RSI (Research Science Institute, MIT)** — free and merit-based. Because it costs the
  student nothing, there is no payment to disclose. Highly competitive.
- **Other summer/online research programs** — some are free or stipended; many charge tuition.
  Any program (or coaching, or lab access) that the student or family PAYS FOR triggers the
  STS payment-disclosure rule. Paid programs are PERMITTED, but the payment must be disclosed,
  or the entry can fail to qualify. See `independence-and-disclosure.md` and route the actual
  form to `sts-rules-wizard`.

A program gets the student INTO a lab. It does not, by itself, produce independence. The
independence still has to be earned by owning a separable sub-question and doing the core
work — exactly as for a cold-email placement.

## Realistic expectations (tell the student up front)

- Cold-emailing ~10 well-targeted PIs: typically 1–3 replies; 0–1 willing to host a HS
  student. A warm introduction (your teacher knows them; you met at a conference) raises the
  reply rate several-fold.
- Timeline: 4–8 weeks from first email to a first meeting; another 4–12 weeks to settle into a
  working relationship. Start early relative to the STS deadline.
- False optimism is a disservice. Plan for non-responses and email more than one PI (one PI
  per email).
